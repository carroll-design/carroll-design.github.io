## Overview

I am independently leading a team of student engineers through the design,
fabrication, and testing of a remotely operated underwater vehicle (ROV). 
The build integrates mechanical, electrical, and control systems into a 
single submersible vehicle.

## The Design

Design choices for the ROV are heavily influenced by budget. Our team's goal
is to keep the vehicle as cost effective as possible, while ensuring that the
final product is fully functional.

## The Frame

The ROV's frame will be made primarily of PVC piping. There are several benefits
to this, including its low cost, ease of fabrication and modification, and the 
inherent buoyancy of the hollow pipes.

## Propulsion

Standard bilge pumps will be used for underwater propulsion. We will use the 
bilge pumps in one of two ways, which will be decided upon after some testing.
First, our team will test the bilge pumps as they come from the factory. If they
produce sufficient directional thrust, then we will leave them in this
configuration and continue to integrate them with the rest of the ROV. 

If the thrust is not satisfactory, we will remove the outer housing of each 
bilge pump, exposing the motor shaft. Propellors will be modelled, 3D-printed,
and attached to each motor shaft, allowing for the bilge pump motors to propel
the vehicle.

There will be three motors total; one mounted vertically for depth control, and
one mounted horizontally on either side of the ROV allowing for forward and
backward movement as well as yaw control.

## Power and Control systems

The entire vehicle will be powered by a 12V 18Ah SLA battery, which will be kept
on the surface. Power will move from the battery into the controller, where three
Double Pole Double Throw (DPDT) switches will determine how power is allocated.
Each motor is to be connected to one of the three DPDT switches via a waterproof
tether that connects the vehicle to the controller. 

For the vertically mounted motor, if the DPDT switch is pushed forward, it will
allow for power to travel through the switch, down the tether, and into the
motor. This would cause the motor to produce downward thrust, and result in the
vehicle moving downward below the water's surface. If the switch was pushed
backward, however, it would reverse the direction of the power travelling into
the motor, and result in the vehicle moving upward.

## Live Video Feed

The feature that I am most excited about in the ROV is the live video that
will allow us to see from the vehicle's point of view. When this was First
brought up, I was worried it would require some tedious software and
electrical work, and work likely end up being expensive. 

After some research, I discovered that there are several companies that sell 
fishing cameras which would be perfect for this project. The camera our team
settled on includes the camera, a video monitor with a built in battery, and 
a 50 foot waterproof cable connecting the two which transmits power and video 
signals.

Being able to implement this off-the-shelf component into the project will
save us time, resources, and money, as opposed to if we were to have to
custom design, assemble, and program our own.

## Current Stage

Our team is currently finalizing the design of the ROV. We have been keeping
track of the project's parts and cost with a Bill of Materials, a practice
that I picked up during my internship with AeroVironment. Once the design is
finalized all parts will be ordered, and fabrication of the ROV can begin.
